Most high-growth SaaS companies don't have a growth problem. They have a unit economics problem wearing a growth costume.

I was reviewing financial models with my co-founder Dare late one evening, evaluating a prospective SaaS acquisition. On paper, the company looked like a rocket ship — ARR had doubled from $1M to $2M in twelve months. "Look at this," Dare said. "They're crushing it."

I pulled their cohort telemetry. Five minutes later: "Dare, this company isn't crushing it. It's on life support. They're spending three dollars on sales and ads for every dollar of recurring revenue, and customers are churning out the back door after four months. They run out of cash by Q3."

Revenue growth is trivially easy to manufacture — throw enough capital at ad networks and sales commissions and the top line moves. If the unit economics underneath are broken, scaling spend doesn't make you profitable. It accelerates the bankruptcy.

## The Unit Economics Scorecard

**The Unit Economics Scorecard is the set of four load-bearing metrics — CAC, LTV, CAC Payback Period, and NRR — that determines whether recurring revenue growth is real or borrowed against future cash.** ARR and MRR tell you the size of the business. They tell you nothing about whether the business is solvent. A company can double ARR and still be structurally insolvent if the cost to acquire that revenue exceeds what it returns.

**MRR (Monthly Recurring Revenue) is the total predictable, normalized subscription revenue generated by active customers in a single month. ARR (Annual Recurring Revenue) is MRR multiplied by 12** — your annual run-rate. Both are baseline heartbeat metrics. Neither one, on its own, tells you if the heartbeat is healthy.

**The key insight:** Revenue growth is a vanity metric until you run it through the Scorecard. A rocket ship and a company on life support can post the exact same ARR chart.

## CAC: What a Customer Actually Costs You

**Customer Acquisition Cost (CAC) is the total fully loaded cost required to acquire a single new paying customer** — every dollar of sales and marketing spend divided by the number of new customers acquired:

CAC = (Total Sales Spend + Total Marketing Spend) ÷ Number of New Customers Acquired

The fully loaded part matters. Ad spend alone isn't CAC. You must include software stack subscriptions, sales commissions, and team salaries — anything that exists to acquire the customer. Founders who calculate CAC from ad spend alone are underreporting it, often by half.

**The key insight:** An understated CAC is worse than no CAC at all, because it tells you a channel is profitable when it isn't. Instrument every cost line before you trust the number — see [how a lead qualification pipeline keeps CAC honest](https://clintjeez.com/writings/lead-qualification-gateway-icp-mql-sql) by filtering low-intent leads out before sales spends a cycle on them.

## LTV and CAC Payback Period: The Time-to-Solvency Metrics

**Lifetime Value (LTV) is the total gross profit margin a customer generates over their entire lifespan with your company:**

LTV = (Average Revenue Per Account × Gross Margin %) ÷ Churn Rate

LTV tells you the total prize. It doesn't tell you when you actually get paid. That's a separate metric.

**CAC Payback Period is the number of months it takes a new customer to generate enough gross profit to fully repay the CAC spent to acquire them:**

CAC Payback Period (Months) = CAC ÷ (ARPA × Gross Margin %)

A $1,200 CAC against a $100/month customer at 100% gross margin pays back in exactly 12 months. Every month before that, you're operating in the red on that customer — cash already spent, not yet recovered.

![CAC Payback Period timeline showing months 0–12 as red (recovering acquisition cost) and months 12+ as green (pure gross profit)](/infographics/article9-cac-payback-timeline.svg)

**The key insight:** Every month under your payback period is runway you've already spent and haven't gotten back. A payback period over 18 months means you're funding growth out of cash reserves, not out of the customers you're acquiring.

## NRR: The Holy Grail Metric

**Net Revenue Retention (NRR) measures the percentage of recurring revenue retained from an existing customer cohort over a period, accounting for expansion, downgrades, and churn:**

NRR = (Starting MRR + Expansion MRR − Downgrade MRR − Churned MRR) ÷ Starting MRR × 100

Below 100% NRR, existing customers are shrinking your revenue faster than they expand it — you're on a treadmill, forced to acquire new customers just to stay flat. Above 100% — net negative churn — your existing cohort spends more with you every year through seat upgrades and cross-sell, and the business compounds even at zero new acquisition spend.

![NRR compound growth comparison — the same $100K starting cohort tracked over 3 years at under-100% NRR versus over-120% NRR, with no new acquisition spend](/infographics/article9-nrr-compound-growth.svg)

**The key insight:** NRR is the one metric that separates companies from legendary compounding platforms. Same starting cohort, zero new spend, two completely different three-year outcomes — the only variable that moved was retention and expansion.

An LTV:CAC ratio below 1:1 means you lose money on every customer you acquire — no amount of volume fixes that math. A payback period over 18 months and NRR under 90% together mean you're funding a leaky bucket with fresh capital instead of fixing the leak.

**The key insight:** These four numbers are a gate, not a report. If any one of them sits in the Danger Zone, scaling spend doesn't grow the business — it scales the exact problem you haven't fixed yet.

## Real-World Diagnostic

**Before:** The acquisition target had doubled ARR from $1M to $2M in twelve months. On the surface — the only surface most buyers check — it looked like a clean growth story worth a premium multiple.

**The diagnosis:** Running the Scorecard against their cohort telemetry told a different story. Sales and marketing spend sat at roughly 3x recurring revenue generated — deep in Danger Zone territory on any CAC-to-revenue read. Average customer lifespan was four months, which meant LTV was collapsing the CAC Payback Period to something the business could never actually recover within a customer's tenure. The revenue growth wasn't organic compounding — it was capital being converted into a shrinking, leaking cohort.

**The fix:** We priced the acquisition on the real unit economics, not the ARR chart — which meant the asking valuation no longer made sense without a costly retention rebuild neither side had budgeted for.

**The result:** We passed on the deal. A revenue chart that looks like a rocket ship on the outside and scores Danger Zone on the Scorecard isn't a growth story. It's a cash problem with better marketing.

## Engineering Your Unit Economics

You don't fix unit economics by editing a spreadsheet. You fix them in the product and the revenue architecture — the same systems discussed across this series.

**To reduce CAC:** Automate your qualification pipeline so reps stop spending cycles on low-intent leads — see [the Lead Qualification Gateway](https://clintjeez.com/writings/lead-qualification-gateway-icp-mql-sql) — and match your growth motion to your actual ACV instead of overspending on the wrong channel, per [the Growth Motion Decision Matrix](https://clintjeez.com/writings/growth-motion-decision-matrix-plg-slg-abm).

**To improve LTV and Payback Period:** Optimize the activation flow to get users to their "aha" moment faster — see [how to diagnose a SaaS activation problem in 48 hours](https://clintjeez.com/writings/find-saas-activation-problem-48-hours) — since every day a user takes to activate is a day added to your payback clock.

**To drive NRR above 120%:** Instrument usage-based pricing triggers — API calls, database rows, active seats — so revenue expands automatically as customers grow, instead of depending on a renewal conversation to capture the upside.

Two mistakes show up constantly:

- **Reporting CAC without fully loaded costs.** Ad spend alone understates CAC and makes broken channels look viable.
- **Treating ARR growth as proof of health.** ARR measures size. Only the Scorecard measures whether that size is solvent.

The meta-lesson: building a durable SaaS company isn't about flash-in-the-pan campaigns. It's engineering a revenue pipeline where every dollar spent on acquisition returns three to five dollars in enterprise value — and you only know that's happening if you instrument the telemetry to check.

## Where to Start

1. Calculate your fully loaded CAC — every cost line, not just ad spend — for your last full quarter of new customers.
2. Calculate your CAC Payback Period and compare it against the Scorecard: under 6 months is elite, over 18 is Danger Zone.
3. Pull your NRR for the last 12 months. If it's under 100%, treat retention as the priority over new acquisition.
4. Instrument cohort-level telemetry in PostHog or your billing system so LTV, churn, and expansion are calculated from real usage data, not estimates.
5. Run every growth spend decision — a new channel, a new headcount, a new campaign — through the Scorecard before you commit budget to it.
6. If any one of the four metrics sits in the Danger Zone, fix that metric before you scale spend on anything else.

Revenue growth is easy to manufacture. Solvent revenue growth is engineered — and the Scorecard is how you tell which one you're looking at.

## FAQ

**What's a good LTV:CAC ratio for a SaaS company?**
A ratio of 3:1 is a solid operational benchmark — you're generating three dollars of lifetime value for every dollar spent acquiring the customer. Below 1:1 means you lose money on every customer acquired. Above 5:1 is elite territory, though it can also signal you're underinvesting in growth.

**What counts as a good Net Revenue Retention rate?**
100–110% NRR is a healthy benchmark for SMB-focused SaaS. Enterprise-focused companies should target above 120%, known as net negative churn, where expansion revenue from existing customers alone drives compounding growth without any new acquisition spend.

**How is CAC Payback Period different from LTV?**
LTV measures the total gross profit a customer generates over their entire lifespan. CAC Payback Period measures how many months it takes to recover the acquisition cost specifically — it's a cash flow timing metric, not a total value metric, and it's the number that determines how much runway a growth strategy actually consumes before it pays for itself.
